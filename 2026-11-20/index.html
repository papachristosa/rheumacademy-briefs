Willingness for TKA, Step Counts and the Thumb Base

By Angelo Papachristos, PT, ACPAC

This week's papers kept showing a gap between what patients tell us and what gets measured or recorded. Self-reported activity in axSpA did not match recorded step counts in most patients who rated themselves active. In a small giant cell arteritis study, low CRP together with absent large-vessel PET uptake was linked to visual impairment, and stated willingness to have knee surgery was more strongly associated with a surgical recommendation in men than in women. The hand papers gave OTs long-term figures to quote on both conservative and surgical options.

1.Willingness was more strongly associated with a TKA recommendation in men than in women

5.03 versus 2.15. Those are the adjusted risk ratios for a 'definitely willing' patient being recommended total knee arthroplasty, in men (95% CI 3.04-8.33) and women (95% CI 1.68-2.75). The data come from a cross-sectional study of 2,035 patients, nested in an Alberta cohort of people with knee OA referred to hip and knee surgery clinics between 2014 and 2017. Women also had lower adjusted odds of being willing (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51-0.88). The authors present the difference as an association that raises concern about bias at the point where willingness becomes a surgical recommendation.

AHP Takeaway: If you triage knee OA referrals, record willingness and functional status in the same structured format for every patient before the surgical consult. The surgeon then receives comparable information regardless of the patient's sex.

Source: Journal of Rheumatology · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42880894/

2.Nearly one in five dermatomyositis patients had peripheral neuropathy

Consider a woman with classic dermatomyositis who reports numb feet and new unsteadiness on stairs while her CK is well controlled. Part of her limitation may be neuropathic. In a single-centre cohort of 640 patients with DM, 120 (18.8%) had clinically documented peripheral neuropathy after secondary causes were excluded, identified by record review at a median of 3 years after diagnosis. Among the 39 patients with positive nerve conduction studies, axonal features predominated (37 of 39). Patients with neuropathy more often had arthralgia (79.2% vs 18.2%, P<.001) and Raynaud syndrome (55.8% vs 38.7%, P<.001) than those without.

AHP Takeaway: Add a brief sensory screen and a balance measure to myositis reassessments. If a strength plateau comes with distal sensory change, flag it for nerve conduction studies before you escalate resistance load.

Source: JAMA Dermatology · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43050210/

3.Cochrane: turmeric may give little to no improvement in knee OA pain or function

Turmeric is the supplement my knee OA patients ask about most often. A Cochrane review of nine trials (1,245 participants, all with knee OA) concluded that, compared with placebo, Curcuma longa may result in little to no improvement in pain, function or quality of life. In the four placebo-controlled trials, pain at three months was 0.72 points lower on a 0 to 10 scale (95% CI 1.39 to 0.06 lower), short of the 1.5 points the reviewers set for clinical significance. The evidence was low certainty, downgraded for indirectness and publication bias, and the evidence on harms was very low certainty. Participant-reported global assessment of success may improve. No trial ran longer than three months.

AHP Takeaway: Tell patients that the best available evidence, low certainty and limited to the knee and to three months, suggests little to no benefit for pain or function. If they want to keep taking it, record it on the medication list and keep loading-based exercise at the centre of the plan.

Source: Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43051256/
